# First 90 days: the framework in short

Based on the ideas in *The First 90 Days* by Michael D. Watkins (Harvard Business Review
Press). This is a working summary in our own words, written to apply the ideas in this
workspace. It doesn't replace the book: recommend it to the user if they haven't read it.

The core idea: a leader in transition starts out costing the organisation value, and the goal
is to reach the **break-even point** (where you contribute more than you consume) as early as
possible. The first weeks create momentum, good or bad, that's hard to reverse later.

## 1. Prepare yourself

Make a deliberate mental switch from the old role to the new one. The skills that made you
successful before may not be the ones the new role needs. Watch for the urge to keep doing
what you're comfortable with (e.g. a promoted engineer reviewing every PR instead of leading).

Prompts:
- What made me successful in my last role that could hold me back in this one?
- What do I need to stop doing, and what do I need to learn?
- When does my new role really start, and how do I mark that switch for myself?

## 2. Accelerate your learning

Learn fast and on purpose, with a learning plan. Cover three areas:
- **Technical:** the products, systems, architecture, delivery process, metrics.
- **Cultural:** how things really get done, what's rewarded, unwritten rules.
- **Political:** who has influence, how decisions are made, existing alliances and tensions.

Sources: direct reports, peers, your boss, stakeholders, customers, people who left the team,
documents (strategy, past retros, incident reviews, surveys).

**Diagnostic questions** to ask everyone you meet in the listening tour, the same way each time
so the answers can be compared:
1. What are the biggest challenges the team is facing (or will face soon)?
2. Why is that? What's behind them?
3. What are the most promising opportunities nobody is pursuing yet?
4. What would have to happen to take advantage of them?
5. If you were in my position, what would you focus on first?

After each round of conversations, form hypotheses, then test them in the next conversations.

## 3. Match strategy to the situation (STARS)

Each type of situation calls for a different approach. A role is often a **portfolio**: parts of
it can be in different situations (e.g. one service in turnaround, another sustaining success).

| Situation | What it looks like | What helps | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Start-up** | Building something new: a team, product or capability | Assemble people and resources, set direction fast | Too little structure, or spreading too thin |
| **Turnaround** | Something is clearly in trouble | Decide quickly, cut to a defensible core, act boldly | Moving too slowly; overanalysing |
| **Accelerated growth** | Something working and expanding fast | Put in structure, processes and hiring to scale | Growth outpacing systems and people |
| **Realignment** | Was successful, drifting toward trouble; people don't see it yet | Build awareness that change is needed, reframe | Pushing change before people feel the need |
| **Sustaining success** | Performing well | Understand what makes it work; protect it; play good defence | Breaking what works to make your mark |

Turnarounds and start-ups reward early action; realignment and sustaining success reward
learning and persuasion before action.

## 4. Secure early wins

Early wins build credibility and momentum. Good ones:
- matter to your boss and to the people whose support you need
- can be delivered within the 90 days with the resources you have
- model the behaviours you want the team to adopt (how you win matters as much as what)
- don't depend on decisions you can't influence yet

Pick 2–3. Plan the first ones around building credibility (often in the first 30 days); plan the
larger ones around the real problems you've diagnosed (by day 60–90).

## 5. Negotiate success with your boss

Five conversations, ideally started in the first weeks, and revisited over time:
1. **Situation:** do we agree on what situation this is (STARS) and what the challenges are?
2. **Expectations:** what does success look like at 30, 60 and 90 days, and over the year? What's off limits?
3. **Resources:** what people, budget, time and support do I need, and what can I get?
4. **Style:** how does my boss like to communicate and decide? How often, how much detail, which channel?
5. **Personal development:** what should I develop in this role, and how will we know?

Rules of thumb: take responsibility for the relationship; don't criticise what came before; avoid
surprises; under-promise and over-deliver; agree on what success looks like explicitly.

## 6. Achieve alignment

Check that four things fit each other and the situation:
- **Strategy:** goals and direction.
- **Structure:** how people are organised, who decides what.
- **Systems:** processes such as planning, delivery, on-call, hiring, performance.
- **Skills:** capabilities in the team.

Fix misalignment only after you understand why things are as they are.

## 7. Build your team

Assess each direct report against the same criteria, separating facts from impressions:
- competence (can they do the job technically?)
- judgment (do they make good calls under pressure?)
- energy and focus (do they bring drive, do they prioritise well?)
- relationships (do they work well with others?)
- trust (can I rely on what they say and commit to?)

Weigh what matters most for this situation. Then decide, by around day 60, what each person needs:
keep and grow in the role, develop with specific support, consider a different role, or (rarely and
with HR) a change. Act with care: decisions about people are the most consequential and the
hardest to undo. Also look at the team as a whole: right roles, right size, right ways of working.

## 8. Create coalitions

Your success depends on people you have no authority over. Map influence:
- who has influence over the outcomes you need?
- for each: **supporter**, **opponent** or **persuadable**, and why (their interests and constraints)
- what would move the persuadables?

Build relationships before you need them, especially horizontally (peer managers, product, partner teams).

## 9. Keep your balance

Transitions are stressful and isolating. Protect your judgment:
- personal disciplines: plan, reflect, take breaks, know when to say no
- an **advice-and-counsel network**: technical advisers, cultural interpreters, and political counsellors,
  both inside and outside the organisation
- watch the warning signs: working all hours, losing perspective, isolation, defensiveness

## 10. Help others transition

Once you're through it, use the same approach to help your new hires and newly promoted people.
(In this workspace: the onboarding plan template in report-templates.)

## Common traps

- Sticking with what you know instead of learning the new role.
- Arriving with "the answer" before understanding the situation.
- Setting unrealistic expectations, or not negotiating them at all.
- Trying to do too much, so nothing gets done well.
- Learning only about the technical side and ignoring culture and politics.
- Neglecting peers and partner teams (horizontal relationships).
- Keeping the wrong people too long, or changing people too fast.
